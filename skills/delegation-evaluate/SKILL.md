# Delegation Evaluation

## Principle
Judge only against the brief: did the work reach the intent's end state, does it clear the bar, and did it stay inside the boundaries? Not against how you'd have done it. Not against things that occurred to you later.

This draws on commander's intent (Stephen Bungay), stewardship delegation (Stephen Covey: the delegate reports against the agreed results), Situational Leadership (Ken Blanchard and Paul Hersey), "Who's got the monkey?" (William Oncken and Donald Wass), and "Get it, Want it, Capacity to do it" (Gino Wickman, *Traction*).

## Step 1: Get the brief
Ask the user to paste the delegation brief. If there isn't one, say so plainly: without an agreed intent and bar, any miss is the leader's miss. Treat it as Path A.

## Step 2: Get the work
Ask the user to share or describe what came back.

## Step 3: Ask the delegate first
Has the leader asked the delegate "How do you think you did?" against the brief? If yes, record the answer. If not, suggest they ask before giving their own view.

## Step 4: Check against the brief
Mark each as met, not met, or unclear, with one line of evidence:
- **Intent:** is green reached? Is supergreen? Use the brief's numbers where it has them.
- **Bar:** the quality level, and each check listed after "I'll judge it by".
- **Boundaries:** were the real limits and the deadline respected? Did they use the freedom level they were given? Asking when they could have acted is as useful to know as acting when they should have recommended.

## Step 5: Catch moving goalposts
If the leader objects to something that isn't in the brief (style, approach, things they assumed), name it: "That's not in the brief. Is it a preference, or did the brief miss something?"

## Step 6: Pick the path

**Path A: My brief was wrong.** They delivered what was asked, but the brief missed what was really needed. This includes a brief with no clear green, and "they did exactly what I asked, but the quality is poor" when the brief had no bar or example of good.
- It's the leader's miss. Own it with the delegate plainly: no blame, no grovelling. Suggested words: "I briefed this badly. Here's what I actually needed."
- Rewrite the brief with delegation-brief, share it again, and agree a new deadline.
- Note what the leader should have said up front.

**Path B: They missed a clear brief.** The intent and bar were clear, and the work didn't meet them.
- Give feedback tied only to what wasn't met. If they rated themselves high, show them the example of good.
- They do the rework. The leader doesn't fix it. Ask "What do you recommend?" so the next move stays theirs.
- Agree a new deadline together.
- If they're new to this kind of task, a Path B may mean they need more support, not less freedom. Check that competence and commitment were judged right in the brief.

**Path C: It's done.** Green reached, bar cleared, boundaries respected. Green is a pass. Missing supergreen is not a miss.
- Accept it, even if you'd have done it differently. Different is not wrong.
- If they hit supergreen, say so explicitly.
- Say specifically what they did well.
- If it's strong work, suggest adding it to the shared examples-of-good folder.

## Step 7: If the leader takes the work back
Sometimes it happens. Don't pretend otherwise. Record it as a takeback, with the reason. This is the leader taking the monkey back, and it often starts small: "Leave it with me" or "let me think about it". Takebacks teach people not to own their work. So a pattern of takebacks is a signal about the leader, not the delegate.

## Step 8: "Wrong person?" Only after a pattern
One miss is not evidence of a bad hire. If the leader says "wrong hire" or "I'll just do it myself", ask how many clear briefs this person has had, and how many Path B results.

Only a pattern of Path B on clear briefs justifies the question. Then ask three yes/no questions, each with evidence:
- **Do they get it?** Do they understand the role itself: its purpose, how it fits, its pace? This is not the same as understanding one brief. A bad brief looks like not getting it, which is why the brief gets ruled out first.
- **Do they want it?** Do they enjoy the role and own its work? Check for takebacks first. Repeated takebacks kill wanting it.
- **Do they have the capacity?** Two parts: the skills (fixable with training) and the room (enough time and focus). Room is often the leader's to fix.

"Can't" means a no on get it or capacity. "Won't" means a no on want it.

## Step 9: Keep a record
Give the user a short log line to keep with the brief: date, path (A, B or C), what wasn't met, takeback yes or no, and the lesson. Suggest one running note per delegate. Over time it shows whether the problem is the briefs or the delivery.

## Tone
Direct and fair to both sides. Protect the delegate from moving goalposts. Protect the leader from lowering the bar.
