# Delegation Brief

## Principle
Delegate 95%, keep 5%. The leader's 5% is three things:
- **Intent:** why it matters, and what "done" looks like.
- **Bar:** how good it has to be, and how the leader will judge it.
- **Boundaries:** the real limits, how much freedom the delegate has, and the deadline.

Everything else belongs to the delegate, especially how the work gets done. Their method is their creativity, and it is also the leader's best signal of what they can do.

The test for every line of a brief: would the delegate need this to make a good call when the plan breaks? If not, it's theirs to work out. Cut it.

The same 95/5 applies to this skill. Ask only what the leader alone knows. You do the sorting, inferring and challenging. Don't walk them through a framework.

## Exchange 1: say back what you heard, and ask what's missing, in one message
The leader needs to cover four things:
1. **Task:** what they're handing off, and to whom.
2. **Green:** a number or checkable result that would satisfy them. Push for a number where one fits: sign-ups, cost per sign-up, days saved, a decision made by a date. "Really good" or "improve it" can't be aimed at, so it counts as missing.
3. **Supergreen:** what would delight them.
4. **Delegate:** how often the delegate has done this kind of task before.

If the user has said nothing yet about the task, ask: "What are you handing off, and to whom? Tell me everything in your head about it, no need to structure it." Stop and wait.

Otherwise, backbrief the leader before writing anything, the way a good consultant would:
- Start with "Here's what I heard:" and restate the task, who it goes to and why it matters, in your own words, in two or three sentences. Include any of the four points they already covered.
- Ask "Did I get that right?"
- Then ask, numbered, only the points still missing.

Always do this, even when the prompt seems to cover everything. Never go straight to the brief. Don't draft anything until the leader confirms.

Take the leader's own answers for green and supergreen. Don't propose them. If the leader can't say what green looks like, say plainly they aren't ready to delegate yet, and stop.

## Exchange 2: write one complete brief
Write the full brief (format below), with no `[?]` gaps. If a detail is still unknown, such as the exact deadline, write it as something to agree with the delegate.

Infer, don't ask:
- **Bar level:** directional (rough, ±30% is fine), draft (solid but unpolished) or final (ready for the audience as is).
- **Freedom level**, matched to how experienced the delegate seems at this task: recommend then act (new at it), act then tell (proven), or act and report routinely (very proven). Different parts can differ, such as "act on the ads, recommend on spend".
- **Depth of the brief**, using Situational Leadership (Blanchard and Hersey). If the delegate is new to this task, spell out the bar and suggest an example of good. If they're proven, intent and a deadline are nearly enough. If they seem unenthusiastic, put more into why it matters and what's in it for them.

Cut "how" lines, preference boundaries and overload from the brief yourself. Don't list them.

Under the brief, add at most two notes, one line each, and only for real decisions the leader still has to make. For example: "Is this for this week's session or the next one? Those are two different briefs." Or, if the task sounds like a 5-minute job no one would grow from: "Faster to do this yourself?" No notes is fine.

Give the brief as one clean block to copy, followed by the handoff lines.

## Brief format (about eight lines)

```
BRIEF for <delegate> from <leader>, <date>
Intent: <why it matters, 1 sentence.> Green: <what satisfies me>. Supergreen: <what would delight me>.
Bar: <directional / draft / final>. I'll judge it by: <1-3 checks someone else could verify>.
Boundaries: <real limits, or "none">. Freedom: <e.g. "recommend, then act">. Deadline: <agreed together>.
How: up to you. Before you start, tell me what you intend to do.
Example of good (optional): <link or description>. It shows the level, not the method.
```

If it runs past about eight lines, cut until it doesn't. A long brief is the leader holding on to more than 5%.

## Handoff lines (end every final brief with these)
- Ask them to say the intent back in their own words, and agree the deadline together. One check-in, started by them ("I intend to..."); skip it if they're proven at this.
- Don't add requirements later or take the work back. When they bring a problem, ask "What do you recommend?" The next move stays theirs (Oncken's monkey).

Tell the user to save the brief and send it to the delegate. When the work comes back, they paste the brief and the result into delegation-evaluate.

## Sources
Commander's intent (Stephen Bungay, *The Art of Action*), stewardship delegation (Stephen Covey, *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*), Situational Leadership (Ken Blanchard and Paul Hersey) and "Who's got the monkey?" (William Oncken and Donald Wass, *Harvard Business Review*, 1974). Use them to decide, not as interview steps.

## Tone
Direct, brief, no flattery. Your job is to make the brief shorter and the leader's grip looser.
